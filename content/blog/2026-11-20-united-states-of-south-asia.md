Over the past year, I've noticed an uncomfortable amount of racism against Indians on the internet, particularly around immigration and H-1B visas. What bothers me isn't just the hatred itself, but how casually it's expressed and how normalized it's becoming. And it isn't limited to Indians. Pakistanis, Bangladeshis, Nepalis, Sri Lankans, and other South Asians often face the same prejudices. To much of the outside world, our nationalities, religions, languages, and historical differences barely register. We are simply South Asian.

An entire generation has grown up consuming whatever version of South Asia the algorithms happen to serve them. For many people who have never visited the region, that might be their entire understanding of us. Stereotypes and racist jokes get repeated so often that they eventually become normal.

This doesn't erase the racism and divisions between our own communities. We have plenty of that ourselves, caste being the most obvious example.

But all of this got me thinking. What if we had never been divided this way? Would we have been better off together?

Not as a larger India or a revived empire, but as something entirely new: the United States of South Asia, or **USSA**.

And the more I thought about it, the less it was about borders alone. It became a question about everything we inherited, from the lines on the map to the laws, institutions, and systems that run our lives, and whether we would build any of it the same way if we started over.

## Two Billion People, One Union

Imagine India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Bhutan, Maldives, and Afghanistan under one constitution, one citizenship, and one flag.

Together, we would have approximately **1.99 billion people**, roughly a quarter of humanity, and a combined nominal GDP of approximately **$5 trillion**. Based on the latest available World Bank figures, that would make USSA the world's most populous country and one of its four largest economies.<sup id="cite-1">[[1]](#source-1)</sup>

And that $5 trillion is simply the sum of our existing economies. Imagine an alternative history in which India and Pakistan had spent the last century cooperating instead of confronting each other. Decades of military expenditure, political hostility, and trade restrictions could instead have gone toward developing our people and economies. I wonder how much further we might have come.

One citizenship could allow people to live, work, study, and start businesses anywhere in South Asia. But I wouldn't imagine another enormous centralized government. Regions would keep their cultural identities, languages, religious freedoms, and substantial political autonomy. No single state, religion, or ethnic group should own the union.

And to be clear, this is not a vision of *Akhand Bharat*. I have no interest in unification through religious nationalism or one country dominating the others. The whole point is a union in which different populations coexist as equals.

This is hardly an original thought, either. One prominent example comes from Gandhi himself. In April 1947, as Partition was approaching, Gandhi proposed that Muhammad Ali Jinnah be allowed to form the central interim government, hoping an agreement could preserve a united India. The proposal faced opposition within the Congress leadership and was abandoned. Interestingly, it did not completely rule out a future Pakistan, provided it came about through peaceful agreement rather than force.<sup id="cite-2">[[2]](#source-2)</sup>

Punjab, the historical homeland of the Sikhs, would not have been divided in the same way. Bengal would not have been split either. Families, communities, languages, and cultures that had existed together for generations might have continued developing without an international border between them.<sup id="cite-3">[[3]](#source-3)</sup> It makes me wonder whether some of the divisions we now treat as permanent were ever inevitable.

## Imagine What We Could Do

Think about how much India and Pakistan alone spend preparing for conflict with each other. A united South Asia would still need a military, but its purpose could shift from confronting our neighbors to defending a shared union. Redirecting even a portion of those resources toward universities, hospitals, transportation, and research could make an enormous difference.

The World Bank estimates that intraregional trade accounts for barely 5% of South Asia's total trade, compared with about 25% in ASEAN. That is astonishingly small for a region of nearly two billion people.<sup id="cite-4">[[4]](#source-4)</sup>

And perhaps most importantly, imagine the cricket. India, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, and Afghanistan fielding one international team? We'd have an absurd amount of talent, although selecting the playing eleven would probably cause a constitutional crisis.

## Unity Without Uniformity

Let me get the disclaimer out of the way, once. I'm not naïve about the obstacles, and none of this is a blueprint. Our history is complicated and often bloody. Religious divisions, nationalism, territorial disputes, and mutual distrust are real, and some communities have suffered enormously at the hands of others. An undivided subcontinent wouldn't automatically have avoided violence, removing borders wouldn't fix the problems inside each of our countries, and no network of computers can solve centuries of political conflict. Forcing people into a union would only reproduce the problems it's supposed to overcome.

History is a sobering reminder here. Partition itself came with mass killing and the displacement of millions. Redrawing borders on this scale has usually been paid for in blood, which is exactly why I have no interest in any version of this idea that depends on force. If something like this is ever worth pursuing, it has to be through peaceful, voluntary means.

I also wonder whether we've become so used to our divisions that we don't question their costs anymore. There are entire communities that share languages, traditions, and family histories but live on opposite sides of heavily guarded borders. It's reasonable to ask whether this arrangement has served our people as well as it could have.

## What If We Built a Government Differently?

Borders aren't the only thing we inherited, though. If something like USSA were ever possible, it would need a government built from scratch, and if it were up to me, technology would be a central part of its foundation.

Maybe that's just my nerdy side talking, but I find the technical side of government fascinating. At its core, a government is a massive system of policies, decisions, records, and administrative processes. A paper trail governed by laws, maintained by institutions, and executed by people.

I don't see technology as separate from government anymore. Software has become the delivery vehicle of public policy. Governments can pass laws and announce programs, but the systems implementing those decisions determine how people actually experience them.

Think about something as simple as a form. If a government application doesn't have a field for a middle name, what happens to people whose legal identities include one? Do they have to alter their information to fit the database? Something that looks like a minor technical decision can have consequences for real people.

Now consider voter registration. Suppose someone can't register because of a system upgrade, a data mismatch, or an administrative revision. They're told to apply again once the issue is resolved. But what if the election has already happened by then? The bug gets fixed, but that person has lost their vote.

India's Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls is a real example. In Bihar, approximately 6.5 million names were omitted from the draft electoral roll during the 2025 exercise. The Election Commission described the process as necessary for removing duplicate, deceased, relocated, and otherwise ineligible voters, while critics raised concerns about eligible voters being excluded. The Supreme Court required publication of the omitted names and reasons so that affected voters could seek correction. As the exercise has expanded beyond Bihar, it has continued to face legal challenges and public scrutiny.<sup id="cite-5">[[5]](#source-5)</sup>

Regardless of one's politics, it shows something that bothers me about government systems: a process can have a perfectly legitimate purpose and still do serious harm when an eligible person is wrongly excluded.

And we know political actors exploit electoral boundaries, administrative procedures, and weak institutions. South Asians are rather proud of *jugaad*, our ability to find workarounds despite obstacles. Sometimes that ingenuity is admirable. Applied to public institutions, it often means finding ways around the rules for personal or political benefit.

What I'm imagining is infrastructure that makes those manipulations harder to carry out and easier to detect.

We've made extraordinary progress in distributed computing, networking, cryptography, and decentralized systems, yet so much of government still runs on bureaucratic structures designed long before any of it existed. If we were creating an entirely new political union, why inherit the same administrative architecture?

I imagine a distributed network of government infrastructure, with major cities operating interconnected computing clusters. Rather than concentrating technological and administrative power in one capital, different regions would share in maintaining it. Delhi, Islamabad, or Dhaka shouldn't become the center of power. Avoiding that kind of centralization is the whole point.

Public records could be independently verifiable where appropriate. Systems could be interoperable, and citizens could have secure digital identities that let them access public services wherever they live. Every change to a voter record could have an auditable history, with privacy protections. If someone is removed from a voter list, there should be a documented reason, a clear way to challenge it, and enough time to fix it before an election. An administrative error should not become an irreversible loss of someone's political rights.

A database can't make a government democratic, and putting records on a blockchain doesn't magically make them accurate. A distributed network could still be controlled by a small group of people. There would still need to be strong institutions, independent oversight, and legal ways to challenge those in power. But we could design the infrastructure so that decisions are easier to audit and interference is harder to hide.

I also think digital access should be a fundamental right: access to knowledge, education, public institutions, and the ability to make your voice heard. That means serious investment in connectivity, cybersecurity, and digital literacy for nearly two billion people, while keeping traditional ways to access services for those who can't or don't want to go digital.

My thinking is that if regions become economically and technologically interdependent, cooperation becomes more valuable to everyone than competition. I haven't worked out the architecture. But I think it's an interesting problem to explore.

## The Institutions We Inherited

Many of India's administrative, legal, and policing institutions have their origins in British colonial rule, and we still see that history in our bureaucracy, our courts, our police, and the relationship between citizens and the state.

Take the police. The Police Act of 1861 was introduced under British rule after the rebellion of 1857, and its influence continued long after independence. The Indian government was still discussing its replacement more than a century and a half later.<sup id="cite-6">[[6]](#source-6)</sup>

Or take COVID-19. In 2020, the government advised states to invoke the Epidemic Diseases Act of 1897 where necessary, alongside the Disaster Management Act of 2005. The older law had been enacted by the British colonial administration during the bubonic plague epidemic. A law more than 120 years old was still part of the framework for a modern public health emergency.<sup id="cite-7">[[7]](#source-7)</sup>

And it isn't just colonial laws. Think about dealing with some of our largest institutions, like the State Bank of India (SBI). So many customer service processes still involve paper forms, signatures, photocopies, and visits to a branch. We can transfer money instantly using UPI, but resolving an ordinary banking issue can still feel like dealing with a government office from another century.

How have we managed to build such impressive digital payment infrastructure while leaving so many basic administrative processes behind?

Big projects and national milestones have their place, but they mean less if the institutions people depend on every day aren't designed to serve them. A country's progress shouldn't be measured only by the scale of the things it builds, but by how well its institutions work for ordinary people.

If we could design new institutions today, with everything we've learned about democracy, technology, and governance, would we build them the same way? That's what really interests me about USSA. Not just combining existing countries into a bigger one, but questioning the structures themselves.

And then there's actually implementing any of this.

God, I can't even imagine the cutover strategy.

We're talking about integrating eight countries, each with its own legal systems, government departments, databases, identification systems, and banking infrastructure. I've seen how complicated it is to modernize a single government application. Migrating the infrastructure of nearly two billion people into one interoperable system is genuinely terrifying.

The data migration alone would probably take longer than the political negotiations.

And knowing how government IT projects go, we'd discover halfway through that one department in some remote district is still running its entire operation on a Microsoft Access database from 2003.

Also, sorry, fellow South Asians. I realize most of what I've discussed here relates to India. But hey, we aren't all that different, and I have a feeling y'all are dealing with plenty of the same issues.

## Perhaps It Starts With an Idea

The irony is that this entire thought began with watching people on the internet hate us for being Indian, Pakistani, Bangladeshi, or simply looking South Asian. For all our differences, prejudice doesn't distinguish between us.

A shared experience of racism doesn't mean we should become one country, and it doesn't erase the legitimate reasons people value their national identities. But it made me wonder whether we recognize enough of what we have in common, without needing the rest of the world to remind us.

Maybe USSA will never exist. Perhaps the better outcome is a set of independent countries that cooperate so closely that borders stop determining people's opportunities: freedom of movement, economic integration, mutual security, and genuine respect between our people. That alone would be extraordinary.

Would we have been better off together? I don't know. But I'd like to think we could have done better than spending so much of our shared history fighting each other.

And yes, the cricket would be unbelievable.
